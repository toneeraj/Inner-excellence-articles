title: Let us not give ourselves a reason
pillar: P3 — Attention Is a Moral Act
originally_sent: 2026-11-08
standfirst: Dogen's cook answers the offer of laborers with "They are not me," which rates nobody and claims nothing, so the dull job can be ours with no sentence about us attached.
sources:

A travelling monk finds an old cook out in the yard at the hottest part of the day, spreading mushrooms on the ground to dry, and asks him why he does not get one of the laborers to do it. It is a kind question. The cook answers it in four words, and the four words are not a reason.

> "Why do you not employ postulants or laborers?"
>
> "They are not me."
>
> — Dogen, Tenzo Kyokun (Instructions for the Cook), 1237, translated by Griffith Foulk

The monk's question carries an assumption and the assumption is sound. Mushrooms dry the same under anyone's hands. If the work is the dried mushrooms, then the hands are a detail, and the only thing left to settle is which hands are cheapest to use. The cook does not dispute that the mushrooms would dry. He is asked about hands and he answers about who is standing in the yard.

Look at what his answer leaves out. There is no assessment of the laborers in it — not that they are careless, not that they are busy, not that they would get it wrong. Any reason he could have given would have had to rate them. He gives none, and that is also what keeps the answer from being a claim about himself. Nobody is being measured in those four words.

The monk's question is the one that forms in us when a dull job appears and somebody else could do it. We answer it. I'm the only one who'll do it properly. It isn't my job. I don't mind, I'll finish it. Nobody else is going to. Every one of those is true enough, and every one of them is about us.

Giving the reason is what happens on its own, and the reason is usually accurate. What it costs is that a reason has an audience. It is addressed to the person who offered, and once it is out it sits between the two of you as a small position to be accepted or doubted. The rest of the job goes on behind that.

Handing it over is the monk's own suggestion and it is frequently the right thing to do. The laborers exist, the work gets finished, and nothing is lost on the mushroom side. What it costs is the hour, and that is the one cost that leaves no evidence. The mushrooms come out dry either way, so there is nothing afterwards to look at that would show anything had gone missing.

Taking the cook's answer costs two things. It does not transfer — *they are not me* settles nothing about when work should be handed over and when it should not, so there is nothing in it to carry into tomorrow. And it cannot be said out loud. Four words in a yard in the thirteenth century; said at your own sink it comes out as vanity or as complaint, and it is neither of those, so there is no version of it you can offer to the person who asked. What is left is the hour, which is a few mushrooms wide and over by evening.

So what are we aiming at. We want the dull job to be ours with no sentence about us attached to it.

*Let us see where this shows up in an ordinary day. Three places.*

- Somebody at home offers to take over the washing-up, and you hear the explanation starting.
- There is a small job in the house you could pay someone to do, and you are weighing the afternoon it would cost.
- Someone offers to make a phone call you were about to make yourself.

One thing to watch in all three: the sentence that forms in the second after the offer. Is there a reason about you in it, or only the fact that you are the one here.

You are not deciding anything in that second, and you are not trying to turn the offer down. Watch what the sentence is made of, and leave it as it is.
