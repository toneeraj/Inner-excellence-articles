Somebody tells you something is off, and your first question is out before you have thought about it. It is asked out of real interest, and it has an answer folded inside it. Simone Weil names one question with nothing folded inside it at all.

> The love of our neighbour in all its fullness simply means being able to say to him: "What are you going through?"
>
> The soul empties itself of all its own contents in order to receive into itself the being it is looking at, just as he is, in all his truth.
>
> — Simone Weil, "Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies with a View to the Love of God," 1942

Weil's question has nothing of ours in it. Nothing in *what are you going through* names the trouble, or dates it, or picks a cause. Set it against the questions that actually get asked in that second. Is it the same thing as last week. Did you not sleep. Is this about Sunday. Every one of those arrives with an answer already in it, and every one is asked out of genuine interest in the person, which is exactly why it is invisible. A guess feels like attention.

Asking the guess is what happens on its own, and what it costs is the part of their situation that was not in the guess. A guess is made of what you already know about them, which is last week's material. People answer the question they were asked — out of politeness, or because the shape offered is close enough to take — so the next ten minutes run on your material and you come away with a confirmation. It will feel like you understood them quickly.

Keeping the guess to yourself and asking something open is the move people make after they have been told once too often that they jump ahead. The guess does not leave. It goes under, and becomes the thing the listening is organised around: you hear the parts that bear on it, and the rest goes past as preamble. A guess held quietly also gets confirmed more easily than one said out loud, because they never got the chance to tell you it was wrong.

Asking the empty question costs you the shape. What comes back has no particular length and does not have to be about anything you can do something about. There is no credit in having asked it either, because the question was nothing — anybody could have said those five words. And you have to hear what is actually going on, which is sometimes heavier than the guess was and sometimes a great deal more boring.

So what are we aiming at. We want the question to leave us with nothing of ours inside it.

*Let us see where this shows up in an ordinary day. Three places.*

- Someone at home comes in and says the day was bad.
- A friend messages to say something has happened, and nothing else.
- Someone tells you they have decided something, and you already had an idea about why.

One thing to watch in all three: your first question, in the second before it goes out. One question, two answers. Is there an answer already sitting in it, or does it leave the whole thing open.

You are not deciding anything in that second, and you are not trying to ask a better question. Watch what your question already has in it, and leave it as it is.
