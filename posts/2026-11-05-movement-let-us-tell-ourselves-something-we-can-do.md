Vivekananda hands out four orders in one breath, and then he stops giving orders. What comes after them is a different kind of clause, and nobody in it is being told to do anything. Let's look at the place where he stops.

> This rascal ego must be obliterated... Draw near the centre, check desire, stamp it out, let the false self go, then our vision will clear and we shall see God.
>
> — Swami Vivekananda, Complete Works, Vol. 8, "Discourses on Jnana-Yoga"

Four of those verbs are addressed to us. Draw near, check, stamp out, let go. Each one names something a person could start doing in the next second, and each one is small enough to be done badly and still be done. Then comes the word then, and the sentence changes hands. Our vision will clear is in the future tense and its subject is our vision. There is nothing in it for anyone to carry out. He goes further in the last four words, and those we can leave where they are. What matters here is that the orders have run out before the seeing starts.

So the sentence has two halves and only one of them is addressed to us. The other half is the half worth having, and we know it, which is why that is the one we take our instructions from.

Aiming at the second half costs us the first half. Tell yourself to see this person fairly, to be present, to not be so quick, and none of it names an act. So you arrange yourself into something that resembles it, hold the arrangement for about a minute, and then consult yourself about how it is going. The four plain things never got done, because the instruction went straight past them.

Doing the four and then standing at the end of the sentence costs us the four as well, slowly. They stop being things to do and become a payment made in advance. And you are posted at the far end watching for an arrival, which puts you back in the middle of it looking at yourself.

Doing the part addressed to us costs the dignity of the thing. All four orders are subtractions — come nearer, hold the wanting, put it out, let the false one go — and not one of them produces anything you could show anybody. The half that sounds like it matters is not our business, and we are left with four small moves and no view of what they are for.

So what are we aiming at. We want the instructions we give ourselves to be things we could start doing in the next second.

*Let us see where this shows up in an ordinary day. Three places.*

- Someone at the table is telling you about their day, and you tell yourself to be more present.
- Something goes wrong before eight in the morning, and you tell yourself not to let it bother you.
- You are on your way to see somebody you have already made your mind up about, and you tell yourself to keep an open mind.

One thing to watch in all three: the instruction itself, as it arrives. One question, two answers. Is there something in it my hands or my mouth could do in the next second, or does it name how I would like to be feeling.

You are not deciding anything in that second, and you are not trying to produce the better instruction. Watch which kind turns up, and leave it as it is.
