Iris Murdoch is at her window in a bad mood when a kestrel goes past, and the mood goes with it. The going takes no time at all. There is no stretch in her account where she is halfway out.

> "I am looking out of my window in an anxious and resentful state of mind, oblivious of my surroundings, brooding perhaps on some damage done to my prestige. Then suddenly I observe a hovering kestrel. In a moment everything is altered. The brooding self with its hurt vanity has disappeared. There is nothing now but kestrel."
>
> — Iris Murdoch, The Sovereignty of Good (1970)

Everything in that happens at once. In a moment, and then it is gone, so there is no middle to it and no point at which the brooding is three-quarters finished. The only thing in the passage that takes any time is looking out of the window, and she was doing that before the kestrel came.

Handling it is what we do instead, and it works often enough that we keep doing it. Talk yourself down, breathe, walk it off. What that costs is the interval. Working on a mood takes a few minutes, and those minutes are spent in the company of the thing being worked on, which supplies the subject and also supplies the one doing the work. The whole stretch is you and it, and nothing else gets in.

Arguing it down to its real size is the next thing we reach for, and it is honest work. Some injuries are small and look large from the inside. What it costs is that you have to hold the injury up in order to measure it. The measuring needs the thing in full view for as long as it takes, and it can come back the other way: that was large, and here is why. Then you have what you started with, with a case attached to it.

Giving the window to the kestrel costs something too, and it is worth being plain about it. Nothing in that move is addressed to the mood. It is not aimed at the brooding, which is why it can work and also why there is no relief to steer by while you are doing it. And the kestrel is not yours to produce. What you have is a window. Most of what is out of a window is a street, and some days you give it your whole attention and the mood is exactly where it was.

So what are we aiming at. We want to be able to look at something that is not us while the mood is still on, rather than once we have dealt with it.

*Let us see where this shows up in an ordinary day. Three places.*

- You come in from a conversation that went badly and put the kettle on, and the handling starts before the water is hot.
- Someone in the car says look at that, and you are in the middle of something.
- You are walking somewhere with a grievance running, and there is a dog on the path in front of you.

One thing to watch in all three: where the attention is in the second something outside offers itself. Is it on the thing out there, or still on the handling.

You are not deciding anything in that second, and you are not trying to make the mood go. Watch where the attention is, and leave it as it is.
