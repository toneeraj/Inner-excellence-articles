A slow half hour ends and something in you writes it up. At least I got some thinking done. That was twenty minutes of my life. David Foster Wallace put two different names on a stretch of time like that, one after the other, and he put them in an order. Let's look at his order.

> "The supermarket is very crowded, because of course it's the time of day when all the other people with jobs also try to squeeze in some grocery shopping. And the store is hideously lit and infused with soul-killing muzak or corporate pop..."
>
> "If you really learn how to pay attention, then you will know there are other options. It will actually be within your power to experience a crowded, hot, slow, consumer-hell type situation as not only meaningful, but sacred."
>
> — David Foster Wallace, "This Is Water," Kenyon College commencement address, 2005

Wallace names two readings of the same half hour and puts them in sequence. Meaningful is a claim about what the time did — it counted, something came of it. Sacred is a claim about the thing in front of you, and there is no me in it anywhere. The words *not only... but* say the second one sits further out than the first, which makes meaningful the near word: the best reading available while I am still the one being paid.

The plain reading of a slow queue is that it was a slow queue. Nothing to collect, nothing came of it, and that is accurate. It also hands the half hour to the room. You are left waiting for a better room, and most rooms are this one.

Meaning has to come from somewhere, so calling the stretch meaningful holds it open until something arrives — a thought, a bit of patience, something you noticed. The stretches that produce nothing go back to being dead time, so the reading only covers the ones that pay. And the sentence it ends in has you in it: what I got, what I learned, what I did with it. The half hour gets described by its deposit.

Wallace does not say how the second word becomes available, and he does not hand it over. He makes it conditional — if you really learn how to pay attention — and what he promises is that you will *know*, not that you can decide to say it. So there is nothing to do with the second word today. Saying sacred about a checkout line while meaning meaningful is just the first word said louder.

So what are we aiming at. We want a dull half hour that owes us nothing.

*Let us see where this shows up in an ordinary day. Three places.*

- A long checkout line finally moves, and on the way to the car you tell yourself what the wait was good for.
- Twenty minutes in traffic, and the moment you get out of the car you account for them.
- A wait at a counter where nothing at all got done, and the stretch goes down as wasted.

In all three the thing to watch is the sentence that arrives at the end, and it has two answers. Is that sentence about the half hour, or is it about what the half hour gave me.

You are not deciding anything in that second, and you are not trying to leave yourself out of the sentence. Watch which of the two it is, and leave it as it is.
