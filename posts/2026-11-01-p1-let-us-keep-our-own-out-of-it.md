Iris Murdoch, a British novelist and philosopher, tells a story about a woman who cannot warm to her daughter-in-law. The complaints are specific and they are all about the girl. Then one more sentence arrives and the woman's son is in it. That sentence is where the complaints were coming from.

> M finds her daughter-in-law D "unpolished and lacking in dignity and refinement. D is inclined to be pert and familiar, insufficiently ceremonious, brusque, sometimes positively rude, always tiresomely juvenile." M feels that her son "has married beneath him."
>
> — Iris Murdoch, "The Idea of Perfection," The Sovereignty of Good (1970)

Every item on the list is something a person could watch D do. Pert, familiar, brusque, juvenile — these are claims about how she talks and how she carries herself in a room, and a stranger standing nearby could agree or disagree with each one. The last sentence is not that kind of claim. Beneath needs two people and a height, and the one being given a height is the son. Nothing on the list gets you there. To arrive at beneath you have to have had the two of them side by side the whole time, which means the list was never a description of D. It was the low reading on a measurement of somebody else.

The son is M's. So the verdict on D is carrying a position about one of M's own, and M has something invested in where he stands. That is what makes a view of a person hard to shift. It is held at the far end, by something that is not D at all.

Settle it on the facts, then. Find out whether D really is brusque, correct the items that are wrong, and let the verdict follow the evidence. The cost is that you can win every item and the verdict will still be standing, because the items were never holding it up. What the examination produces is the same verdict with evidence behind it, which is heavier than what you started with.

Treat the complaints as a cover story and throw them out. The cost is that they were honestly meant. M is reporting what she sees, and she goes back to it later and looks again, which nobody bothers to do with a story they know they invented. Calling the list a lie explains nothing about how an honest person arrives at a word like beneath, and it leaves you with a charge you will be laying against everyone's opinions from now on, the accurate ones included.

Take it that the list is honestly meant and is also a measurement. The cost is that the scale is not visible from where she is standing. What is visible is the list, and the list is not false. So this position hands you nothing at all to do about the complaints. It hands you one thing to check, which is whether the verdict works only as a placement. And it costs something small and real: the plain business of not caring for someone because of how they behave now has a second question attached to it.

So what are we aiming at. We want our opinion of a person to be about that person and nobody else. Not a warmer opinion of them. The same opinion, with none of ours in it.

*Let us see where this shows up in an ordinary day. Three places.*

- Someone close to you describes a new person in their life, and your view of that person is finished before they are.
- A relative has made a choice — a move, a job, someone they have taken up with — and the opinion you have of the choice includes where it leaves them.
- At a family table somebody's partner says something, and the word you reach for puts them somewhere instead of saying what they did.

One thing to watch inside all three: whether there is anybody in the verdict besides the person it is about. Is this a description of them, or is it a placement.

You are not deciding anything in that second, and you are not trying to make the verdict come out fair. Watch whether someone of yours is in it, and leave it as it is.
