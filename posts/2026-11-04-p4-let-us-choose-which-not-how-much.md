When something starts to weigh too much, the move we reach for is to care about it less. Wallace has a sentence that puts that move out of reach. Let's take the word *only* in it seriously and see what it leaves us.

> There is no such thing as not worshipping. Everybody worships. The only choice we get is what to worship.
>
> — David Foster Wallace, "This Is Water," Kenyon College commencement, 2005

Wallace is describing a quantity that does not move. Something in your life is bearing weight at every hour of the day, and he is saying there is no arrangement of a life in which nothing is. What he hands us is a single variable, and it is the identity of the thing.

Turn it down, and the pressure does drop. That part is real, and it is what makes this one hard to catch. But nothing has left the day — it has gone across to something else, and because you did not pick where, you have no way of looking at what took it. The new one carries the weight unnamed, which is the condition the passage is about.

Leave the question alone, on the grounds that arranging your own allegiances is more self-management and therefore more self. The weight still has to sit somewhere, and what settles it then is whatever is nearest and loudest that week. You get a worship, and you did not choose it.

Take the choice where Wallace puts it, and name the thing. You give up travelling light, because there is no version of the day with nothing load-bearing in it — you are never reducing, only ever swapping. And a named thing can be held against the hours in a way an unnamed one never can. The hours will not agree with the naming.

So what are we aiming at. We want our one choice spent on which thing, because which thing is the only choice we are offered.

*Let us see where this shows up in an ordinary day. Three places.*

- Something you were waiting on comes back as a no, and within a minute you hear yourself say it was not that important anyway.
- You are lying awake over a small thing, and you start putting it in perspective.
- Before something you mind about, you tell yourself in advance that you are not going to mind how it goes.

In all three, watch the sentence that lowers the weight, in the second it arrives. One question, two answers. Did anything about the situation change when that sentence turned up, or only how much it weighs.

You are not deciding anything in that second, and you are not trying to stop the sentence coming. Watch what moves, and leave it as it is.
