Somebody tells you what is wrong with something you made, and the thing drops in your own estimation while they are still speaking. You have not been back to look at it yet. Marcus Aurelius asks one question with praise on one side of it and blame on the other, and he gives them the same answer.

> Neither worse then nor better is a thing made by being praised.
>
> Which of these things is beautiful because it is praised, or spoiled by being blamed? Is such a thing as an emerald made worse than it was, if it is not praised? or gold, ivory, purple, a lyre, a little knife, a flower, a shrub?
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, Book IV.20, translated by George Long

The two halves of that question are not the same thing to be on the receiving end of, and the difference is what each one arrives carrying. Praise carries no instruction. You can let it go past and owe nothing to it. Blame arrives as a report — something is wrong here — and a report cannot be let past, because it might be accurate. So it comes in, and in the second it comes in, the report and the thing arrive as one object.

Pull them apart and there are only two cases. If the remark is wrong, nothing happened; there was nothing in it for the thing to receive. If the remark is right, the fault was in the thing before anybody opened their mouth, and it is exactly the size now that it was then. Nobody put it there by saying it. In both cases, what moved in that second was not the thing.

Taking it as damage is what happens on its own, and it is hard to catch because it looks like taking criticism well. What it costs is the thing. You go back to it later and it is worse than it was before anyone spoke, which means you are not looking at the thing any more, you are looking at the thing with a remark attached. And parts of it get changed that were never wrong, because what is being answered is the remark.

Holding that the thing is fine is the other move, and people who have been read unfairly before reach for it first. What it costs is the accurate remarks. They arrive in the same envelope as the inaccurate ones, and a standing position cannot open one and not the other. It also has to be kept up, which makes it something you are doing about yourself inside a conversation that was about a thing.

Letting it stay a claim about the thing costs the whole business of receiving it. Nothing in it is about you, so there is nothing to resent and nothing to forgive, and there is no credit in it either — you do not get to be the one who took it well. And when the remark turns out to be right, you go and do the work, which is the same work it would have been if you had found it yourself.

So what are we aiming at. We want the thing to come out of that second exactly as big as it went in.

*Let us see where this shows up in an ordinary day. Three places.*

- You cook something and somebody at the table says what is wrong with it.
- You send a message and the reply tells you how it came across.
- You pick the route, or the place, or the film, and afterwards somebody says it was the wrong pick.

One thing to watch in all three: the thing itself, in the second after the remark lands. One question, two answers. Is it different now from what it was a minute ago, or is it the same thing with a remark on it.

You are not deciding anything in that second, and you are not trying to take it well. Watch whether the thing moved, and leave it as it is.
