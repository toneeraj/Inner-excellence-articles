Suzuki has one sentence about what happens to a mind that knows a subject well. He is not saying that mind is wrong. He is counting something else, and the number has gone down.

> "In the beginner's mind there are many possibilities; in the expert's mind there are few."
>
> — Shunryu Suzuki, Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind, 1970

The sentence measures possibilities, not accuracy. Someone who has seen a thing five hundred times is usually right about it, and that is exactly what makes him the one people ask. Suzuki is pointing at something underneath the rightness — how wide the field is that the mind is working in when the answer forms.

Look at what the beginner pays for his many possibilities. He is slow. He opens doors that lead nowhere, he holds three readings of one small thing, he takes an hour to arrive somewhere another person reaches in a minute. That cost is real, and nobody would choose it on purpose.

Now look at what is paid on the other side. The answer comes fast and it comes before the looking has finished. The thing in front of you gets sorted into its kind, and once it has a kind it stops being this one — this person, this morning, this particular version of a thing you have met many times. What arrives is the category, wearing the person's name.

Suzuki did not say the expert's mind has none. He said few. Few is not zero, and the whole sentence turns on that. One possibility is the dangerous number, because one possibility does not feel like a possibility at all. It feels like the situation itself.

So what are we aiming at. We are not trying to know less, and we are not trying to be slow again. We want a second possibility to still be standing in the room when the answer comes out — not chosen, not weighed against the first, just there.

*Let us see where this shows up in an ordinary day. Three places.*

- Someone at home begins describing a problem, and by the third sentence you know which problem it is.
- A child explains why something happened, and the explanation is one you have heard from them before.
- A friend asks what you think, and the answer is ready before the question has finished.

The one thing to watch in all three is the count. Not whether the answer was right — it very likely was. Just how many possibilities were in the room at the moment it arrived. One, or more than one.

You are not deciding anything in that second, and you are not trying to make a second possibility appear. Notice the count, and leave it as it is.
