There is a small piece we put on top of a thing after it is finished. The line about how long it took. The mention of what it cost to get hold of. Marcus Aurelius has a short list of things that take no such piece, and the list is the argument. Let's read it.

> That which is really beautiful has no need of anything; not more than law, not more than truth, not more than benevolence or modesty. Which of these things is beautiful because it is praised, or spoiled by being blamed? Is such a thing as an emerald made worse than it was, if it is not praised? or gold, ivory, purple, a lyre, a little knife, a flower, a shrub?
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, Book IV.20, translated by George Long

Marcus's claim is about need. A thing that is really what it is requires nothing further in order to be that. Nothing is missing from it that somebody else, or you a moment later, could go and fetch.

The four items are how he expects us to check. Law, truth, benevolence, modesty — take any one of them and look for the part that has to arrive from outside. A law is not more binding for being quoted back to you. Something true is not more true for being believed by the room. Two of the four are things we do rather than things that sit there, and he files them with the emerald anyway.

If a finished thing needs the extra piece, then nothing we do is over when we stop doing it. There is a second half, and the second half is made out of other people — their attention, their timing, whether they were in the room at all. The thing gets carried around until somebody takes delivery of it.

Stripping the piece off on purpose is a different addition. Saying nothing, in a way that can be noticed as saying nothing, is the same transaction by a longer road, and it has to be kept up.

Letting the thing need nothing costs the record. A good deal of what we do gets read at less than its weight. People take the short version, the short version is sometimes smaller than the thing was, and there is no way to correct it that is not the extra piece.

So what are we aiming at. We want the thing to need nothing added.

*Let us see where this shows up in an ordinary day. Three places.*

- You fix something at home that has been broken for weeks, and when you say it is done you also say how long it took.
- You give someone a lift, and somewhere in the drive the fact that it was out of your way arrives in the conversation.
- You bring food to someone's table and mention what was hard to find.

In all three, watch the extra piece as it arrives. One question, two answers. Is this part of the thing, or is it the receipt for the thing.

You are not deciding anything in that second, and you are not trying to hold the extra piece back. Watch which of the two it was, and leave it as it is.
