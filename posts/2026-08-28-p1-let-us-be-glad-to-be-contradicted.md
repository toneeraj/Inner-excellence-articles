There is a second you pass through several times a week and never examine, because it goes by fast and it is unpleasant. Someone contradicts you, or talks over you, or dismisses something you said. Vivekananda points straight at that second and says something odd about it.

> "Bless men when they revile you. Think how much good they are doing by helping to stamp out the false ego."
>
> — Swami Vivekananda, "Complete Works" Vol. 8,
> "Discourses on Jnana-Yoga," VIII

The argument is not about being gracious. It is about visibility. The ego is invisible while nothing is pressing on it — you cannot find it by looking, because it is the thing doing the looking. The heat that comes up when you are crossed is the one moment it announces its own position.

Taking the bait costs the obvious thing. You defend, and within a few seconds you are no longer discussing the subject, you are managing your standing, and everyone in the room can tell.

Ignoring it costs something quieter. Letting it pass without looking means the one reliable signal you get goes unread. You stay composed and you learn nothing, and the same heat arrives just as fast next time.

So what are we aiming at. We want to treat the flare as information rather than as an instruction. It tells us where we were holding something. Nobody needs to be thanked for providing it, and Vivekananda's language — stamp out, obliterate — is harder than this practice needs. What is being stamped out is a mistake, not an enemy, and nobody has to wrestle it.

One more voice, because the word "false" in that line is doing a lot of work. David Foster Wallace, an American novelist, said in a commencement talk in 2005 that there is no experience you have had that you are not the absolute centre of. That is not a character flaw and he is careful to say so. It is the factory setting. Which means the flare is not evidence that you are worse than other people. It is evidence that the setting is running, in you, as it runs in everyone, and it has just become briefly visible.

There is a slower version worth knowing about, and it is easier to catch because it lasts. The heat arrives in a second, but the grievance can run for half an hour afterwards, collecting fresh charges the whole way — remembering other things the person has done, building the case. Same machinery, clock stretched.

*Let us see where this shows up in an ordinary day. Three places.*

- The second after someone says "no, that's not right."
- The half hour after a conversation that went badly, while you are walking or driving.
- Reading a message and composing the reply in your head before you have finished reading it.

In all three the thing to watch is the same — the heat, and how quickly it turns into a case. Not whether you were right. How fast the case assembled itself.

You are not deciding anything in that second, and you do not have to bless anybody. Watch the flare arrive, and leave it as it is.
